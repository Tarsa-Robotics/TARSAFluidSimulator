Formulas used

Every value on this page comes from the chain below, computed in order top to bottom. Two steps are solved numerically (a root-finder) rather than a closed-form formula — marked where that happens.

01
Hose cross-sectional area
A = (π / 4) · ID²
02
Hose velocity
vhose = Q / A
03
Reynolds number
Re = ρ · vhose · ID / μ
04
Darcy friction factor
Re < 2300 (laminar):   f = 64 / Re
Re ≥ 2300 (turbulent, Colebrook–White):
1 / √f = −2 · log₁₀( ε / (3.7·ID) + 2.51 / (Re·√f) )

Colebrook–White is implicit in f — solved numerically (bisection, replacing what scipy.optimize.brentq does in the Python reference). The Swamee–Jain explicit formula below is used only to bracket the initial guess, never as the final answer:
f₀ = 0.25 / [ log₁₀( ε / (3.7·ID) + 5.74 / Re⁰⁵&sup9; ) ]²

05
Friction pressure loss (Darcy–Weisbach)
ΔPfriction = f · (L / ID) · (ρ · vhose² / 2)
06
Elevation pressure loss
ΔPelevation = ρ · g · H
07
Effective pressure at the nozzle
ΔPeffective = ΔPrated − ΔPfriction − ΔPelevation

Flagged infeasible when ΔPeffective ≤ 0 — the pump cannot overcome the losses at all.

08
Nozzle exit velocity (Torricelli / orifice equation)
vexit = Cv · √(2 · ΔPeffective / ρ)
09
Jet reaction force (momentum flux)
Fjet = ρ · Q · vexit

The "structural reference" value uses the same formula with vexit evaluated at 2·ΔPrated (safety factor 2 on the rated pressure, not on ΔPeffective) — the worst-case number the hardware must survive, not the operating value.

10
Burst margin
burst margin = Pburst / ΔPrated

Flagged red if below the team's SF = 2 convention.

11
Hose mass
hose mass = w · H

w is a direct datasheet value, never computed from ID/OD/material density — real hose weight includes braid/reinforcement mass a wall-volume calculation would miss. Uses H (climb height), not L: the hose hangs straight from a ground-based reel up to the robot, so only the suspended, vertical portion loads the cables — any slack beyond H sits coiled at the base and isn't part of the carried load.

12
Water mass inside the hose
water mass = ρ · A · H

Same H-not-L reasoning as hose mass above. Kept separate from hose mass on purpose — "total suspended mass" below is the sum of both, relevant for what the robot's cable actually has to carry.

13
Total suspended mass
total = hose mass + water mass
14
Hydraulic power
Phyd = Q · ΔPrated

Fluid power the pump must deliver at its rated pressure and flow, shown in the stat card as both watts and horsepower. This is not shaft/motor power — a real drive motor needs more than this to cover the pump's mechanical and volumetric losses, which aren't modeled here. Other pump-spec numbers like RPM, displacement, or the motor's own rated HP don't enter any formula on this page: they determine Q (which you enter directly) but don't change the downstream hydraulics once Q and ΔPrated are known.

15
Water viscosity vs. temperature
μ(T) — linear interpolation over reference values at 0, 10, 20, 30, 40 °C

Standard CRC reference data, not a fitted curve.

16
Solve mode — 1 target

When you lock one output as the target, the one unlocked input is varied by the same bisection root-finder until the target metric above matches what you typed — i.e. it numerically inverts steps 01–14 rather than using a separate formula.

17
Solve mode — 2 simultaneous targets

With 2 inputs unlocked and 2 outputs locked, this becomes a 2×2 nonlinear system (2 unknowns, 2 equations). Rather than a multi-dimensional Newton solve, it's nested bisection: one variable is bisected on the outside; for each candidate value, the other variable is solved exactly (the same 1-target method above) to hit its own target; the outer bisection then drives its own target to zero too. Not every combination of 2 unlocked inputs and 2 target outputs has a solution — e.g. burst margin depends only on pump rated ΔP among the 5 lockable inputs, so targeting it while that input stays locked is genuinely infeasible, not a solver bug.
